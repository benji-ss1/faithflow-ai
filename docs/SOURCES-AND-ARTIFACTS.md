# Sources and artifacts — work like a historian

Rule 4 in [`AGENTS.md`](../AGENTS.md) says facts only. This is *how*.

A historian does not take a document at face value because it exists. They ask
who wrote it, when, from what, and whether it agrees with everything else. Do
that here. Most of the wrong claims in this project came from trusting a file
that was simply out of date.

---

## The distinction

**A SOURCE is the system itself.** It cannot be out of date, because it *is* the
state. Query it.

**An ARTIFACT is something written *about* the system** — a config file, a
markdown doc, an audit, a changelog, a memory file, a previous agent's summary,
anything I said earlier in the conversation. It was true when written. It may not
be true now.

> **Never state a fact from an artifact alone. Artifacts corroborate; sources
> decide.** If the two disagree, the source wins and the artifact is stale — say
> so, and fix it in the same change.

## Where the sources are

| Question | Source | How |
|---|---|---|
| Is this church a lead / on trial / installed? | Ops CRM `PresentflowOPS` → `churches_pipeline` | SQL, `deleted_at is null` |
| Did they actually run services, and for how long? | `PresentflowAPP` → `audio_sessions` (`duration_sec`, distinct days) | SQL |
| How many machines / what size? | Fly | `fly machines list -a faithflow-audio` |
| Is Fluid Compute on? What region? | Vercel project API | `defaultResourceConfig` |
| Pooler or direct? Connection ceiling? | Postgres | `pg_stat_activity`, `pg_settings` |
| What is deployed right now? | `git log origin/main`, `vercel ls` | not the working tree |

Common artifacts that have **already misled us here**: `fly.toml` (said 1 machine
/ 512MB while two 2GB machines ran), `docs/SCALING_AUDIT.md` (12 days stale, half
its findings already fixed), the checked-out branch (540 commits behind `main`),
and my own earlier messages in the same conversation.

## Dating an artifact — do this before relying on one

1. **When was it written?** `git log -1 --format=%ci -- <file>`, or the date in
   its header. No date at all is itself a warning.
2. **What has happened since?** `git log --oneline --since=<that date>` over the
   paths it describes. Lots of commits ⇒ assume it is stale.
3. **Does it agree with the source?** Spot-check its most load-bearing claim
   against the live system. One disagreement invalidates the whole document for
   fact-stating purposes — it does not mean every line is wrong, it means you no
   longer know which lines are.
4. **Who wrote it and from what?** A doc written *from another doc* inherits its
   staleness. An audit generated by an agent is a snapshot of that agent's
   reading, not a measurement.

**Older than ~2–3 weeks and you are about to act on it → stop and ask:**

> "`docs/X.md` was last updated <date>. I'm about to rely on it for <decision>.
> Is it still accurate, or should I re-verify <specific thing> first?"

## Saying numbers honestly

Say **what you counted**, and the units. Then, separately, what you think it
means — labelled as interpretation.

Worked example, from a real mistake in this project:

- ❌ *"All 9 churches are now at 7 days."*
- ✅ *"9 rows in `church_preferences` are now at 7 days. That is 9 product rows,
  not 9 churches: 2 are demo/test, 1 is an internal account, 2 signed up and
  never ran a service. The CRM shows 3 on trial."*

And again, with usage:

- ❌ *"Christ Embassy: 75 sessions."*
- ✅ *"Christ Embassy: 75 `audio_sessions` rows = 16.2 hours across 5 distinct
  days. But 38 of the 75 are under a minute, so 'sessions' counts mic
  connections and reconnects, not services. Distinct days is the better proxy."*

Rules of thumb:
- A **count** needs its table and its unit. `count(*)` on a table is rows.
- A **total** needs its span. "16 hours" over what period, how many days?
- **Check the distribution before quoting an average.** Half of Christ Embassy's
  sessions were under a minute; the mean hid that.
- Exclude **demo/test rows** (`is_demo`, E2E fixtures, internal accounts) from
  anything business-facing, and say that you did.
- If two records might be the same real-world thing, say **"possible
  duplicate — unverified"** rather than counting them twice or silently merging.

## When the source cannot answer

Some things genuinely cannot be read: a write-only secret, a church's real
intentions, whether it works on a projector. Then:

- Say **"I don't know"**, and say exactly how it could be found out.
- Never fill the gap with a plausible number.
- "Unverified" in a report is a feature. A confident guess gets built on, and the
  next agent inherits it as fact.
